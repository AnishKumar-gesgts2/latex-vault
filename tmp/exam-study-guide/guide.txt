Part 1. Review Sheet
Core Learning Objectives
By the time you take this exam, you should be able to:

 • form vectors from points and use dot products, cross products, projections, and magnitudes correctly;

 • find areas using cross products, especially the area of a triangle determined by three points;

 • write equations of lines and planes in 3D;

 • find the line of intersection of two planes;

 • compute the distance from a point to a plane and find the closest point on the plane;

 • determine whether two lines are parallel, intersecting, or skew;

 • identify basic quadric surfaces from their equations and graphs;

 • differentiate vector functions and find velocity, acceleration, and tangent lines;

 • compute arc length and the arc-length function;

 • find T, N, B, and curvature for a space curve;

 • compute tangential and normal components of acceleration.

To find the closest point on the plane, you do not need to memorize a separate formula. Use this method
instead:

  1. The normal vector to the plane is n = ⟨A, B,C⟩.

  4. Plug that value of t back into the line to get the closest point.

A line perpendicular to the xz-plane must use this normal vector as its direction vector. Through (1, 2, 3),
the parametric equations are
                                     x = 1,     y = 2 + t,     z = 3.
Since two direction components are zero, the usual quotient-style symmetric form is not appropriate. A
symmetric-style description is
                                  x = 1,       z = 3,     y is free.

Common mistake to avoid. Do not say the direction vector is in the xz-plane. A line perpendicular to
a plane points in the normal direction to that plane.

Problem 10(b). The line x = y = z has direction vector

                                                  ⟨1, 1, 1⟩.

So a line through (1, 2, 3) parallel to it has parametric equations

                                 x = 1 + t,      y = 2 + t,         z = 3 + t.

The symmetric equations are
                                           x − 1 = y − 2 = z − 3.

Common mistake to avoid. The line x = y = z does not mean the new line must pass through the origin.
It only gives the direction ⟨1, 1, 1⟩.
